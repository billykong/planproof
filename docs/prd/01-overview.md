# 01 - Overview

## Problem

AI agents can now write operations plans: drain these nodes, roll out this image, scale these services. Reviewing those plans is the bottleneck, and it fails in two ways:

1. **Humans can't check multi-step plans reliably.** Whether a ten-step node drain keeps every disruption budget at every step depends on pod placement, ordering and timing. A reviewer reading YAML guesses. At volume, they rubber-stamp.
2. **Rules on single changes miss the rest.** A policy engine that judges each change's effect (as in `safe-ops-agent-k8s`) can't see intermediate states of a multi-step plan. It also can't tell whether the plan does what was asked: in that project's seeded test, 2 of 5 bad proposals were intent-versus-effect mismatches that no rule caught.

Asking a second model to review the plan moves the problem: now the human has to trust that model.

## Product

**planproof is a small, trusted checker (a "kernel") for operations plans that AI agents propose.** It applies the idea behind proof assistants such as Lean: the clever, untrusted part (the agent) does the hard search and writes a plan; a small, simple part (the kernel) checks it. People trust the kernel because they can read it.

- The agent writes a **plan** in a small typed language: a **goal** (exactly what will change) and the **steps**.
- The **kernel** checks the plan against a snapshot of the cluster and a policy. It simulates every step, checks rules at every intermediate state, and checks that the plan's effect equals the goal and nothing else. It answers **Proved**, **Refuted** (with the step and the rule that failed) or **Unknown** (outside what it can reason about).
- The kernel's answer is shown to a person as a **review packet** in a controlled language modelled on ASD-STE100 (Simplified Technical English), generated from templates, never written by a model.
- A separate **executor** turns a Proved plan into API calls in a fixed way and runs it with runtime checks.

The human reads the goal and the packet, not the plan. The kernel is reviewed once.

Kubernetes first, on kind, so anyone can reproduce it.

## Audience

| Who | What they need from it |
| --- | --- |
| Platform and SRE engineers who approve agent changes | A packet they can read in a minute, and a clear statement of what was and was not checked |
| Engineers building agent platforms | A plan format, a checker and an executor contract they can copy |
| Security reviewers | A small trusted computing base, with every assumption written down and tested |
| The author | A demonstrable answer to "how do you trust what the agent did?" |

## Goals

1. **G1 - A small kernel people can read.** The kernel is a pure function, standard library only, with a hard size budget. A competent engineer can review all of it in an afternoon.
2. **G2 - Sound, not complete.** Under its model, the kernel never accepts a plan that breaks a rule. When it can't decide, it says Unknown.
3. **G3 - Check the goal, not only the rules.** The plan's computed effect must equal its stated goal exactly. The human's only per-change judgement is "is this goal what the ticket asked for?".
4. **G4 - Output a person understands and trusts for the right reasons.** Every verdict comes with a review packet in a controlled language, generated by code, that states what was checked, what was not, and why.
5. **G5 - Test the model against the real cluster.** Every operation's model is compared with what a real cluster does. The model is where the guarantee can leak, so it gets the most tests.
6. **G6 - Show that it helps.** Measure, with seeded bad plans, what the kernel catches compared with the `safe-ops-agent-k8s` policy gate, and whether reviewers using the packet catch more than reviewers reading the raw plan (measured with AI reviewers; a study with people was dropped).

## Non-goals

- **A general verifier.** A small, fixed set of operations. Anything outside it is Unknown or rejected.
- **A scheduler simulator.** The kernel uses a conservative model and states its assumptions; it does not reproduce kube-scheduler.
- **Proving the agent is right about *why*.** The kernel checks that the plan does what its goal says, safely. Whether the goal is the right goal stays a human decision, made short on purpose.
- **A production product.** A lab on kind. Hardening, HA and multi-cluster are out of scope.
- **Copying ASD-STE100.** The packet follows its writing principles with the project's own word list; the specification itself is not redistributed.

## Design principles

1. **Trust the checker, not the producer.** The agent is untrusted, however capable. Only the kernel's verdict, bound by hashes, can make a plan runnable.
2. **Keep the trusted part small and pure.** No I/O, no network, no clock, no LLM, no third-party code in the kernel.
3. **When unsure, say Unknown.** Rejecting a safe plan costs a human a minute; accepting an unsafe one costs an outage.
4. **Write every assumption down.** Each modelling assumption has an ID, appears in the packet's "Not checked" section when it applies, and has a test against a real cluster.
5. **Generate what people read.** The packet is rendered from the kernel's facts by fixed templates. No model writes text a person relies on.
6. **Fail closed.** A missing policy, a parse error, a hash mismatch or an unknown field gives no Proved verdict.
7. **Test both directions.** Every rule has a plan it rejects and a nearby plan it accepts.
