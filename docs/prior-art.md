# Prior art: the public work planproof builds on

planproof isn't a new idea. It applies old, well-tested ideas from security engineering, theorem proving, programming-language research, AI planning and operations practice to one new kind of input: change plans written by AI agents. This page maps each part of planproof to the public work it comes from, says what we took, and says where we fall short of the original (PRD E-25).

Where we changed something, the change is small. What's ours is the combination, the narrow domain (Kubernetes node and deployment changes) and the measurements in [`results/`](../results/).

## 1. A small checker you can trust

| Public work | The idea | What planproof takes | Where we fall short |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **The reference monitor.** J. P. Anderson, *Computer Security Technology Planning Study*, ESD-TR-73-51, US Air Force, 1972 ([NIST copy](https://csrc.nist.gov/files/pubs/conference/1998/10/08/proceedings-of-the-21st-nissc-1998/final/docs/early-cs-papers/ande72.pdf); [summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_monitor)) | The mechanism that enforces access must be always invoked, tamper-proof, and small enough to be analysed and tested. | The kernel is the one place a plan is judged; the executor runs it again before it acts and checks the verdict's hashes (SEC-05), and it is held to 1,800 lines, standard library only (K budgets, `make budget`). | "Always invoked" is enforced by our executor, not by the cluster: a person with cluster credentials can still bypass it. |
| **Economy of mechanism.** J. H. Saltzer and M. D. Schroeder, "The Protection of Information in Computer Systems", *Proc. IEEE* 63(9), 1975 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/PROC.1975.9939)) | Keep the design as simple and small as possible, so that it can be checked. | A pure function with no I/O, clock, randomness or goroutines; deterministic output. | - |
| **The LCF approach.** M. Gordon, R. Milner, C. Wadsworth, *Edinburgh LCF*, LNCS 78, Springer, 1979 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-09724-4)) | Clever, untrusted automation proposes; a small trusted core is the only thing that can say "proved". | The agent proposes plans; only the kernel produces Proved. The agent can be any model, even a hostile one. | LCF's guarantee is enforced by a type system; ours by a process boundary and a hash check. |
| **The de Bruijn criterion.** H. Barendregt and F. Wiedijk, "The challenge of computer mathematics", *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. A* 363, 2005 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsta.2005.1650)) | A proof assistant's output should be checkable by a small, independent program. | The plan, snapshot, policy and verdict are plain files bound by hashes; anyone can re-check a verdict with `planproof check`. | We have one checker. Lean has several independent ones ([lean4checker](https://github.com/leanprover/lean4checker), [Lean4Lean](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.14064)); a second, independent kernel would be the equivalent here. |
| **Lean's kernel** ([lean-lang.org](https://lean-lang.org/)) | The direct inspiration: tactics and elaborators can be wrong, but every proof term passes a small kernel. | The name "kernel", the Proved / Refuted / Unknown split, and Refuted facts that work like error messages a planner can repair from. | Lean's kernel checks proofs in a logic. Ours checks plans against a model of Kubernetes, which can itself be wrong (section 3). |
| **seL4.** G. Klein et al., "seL4: Formal Verification of an OS Kernel", SOSP 2009 ([paper](https://trustworthy.systems/publications/papers/Klein_EHACDEEKNSTW_09.abstract)) | A kernel of about 8,700 lines of C, proved correct against its specification. | The size target: small enough that full verification is imaginable. | We test the kernel (golden tests, accept-and-reject tests per rule, fuzzing, 95% coverage); we haven't proved it. That is the phase 5 stretch goal. |

## 2. Checking is easier than finding

| Public work | The idea | What planproof takes | Where we fall short |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Proof-carrying code.** G. C. Necula, "Proof-Carrying Code", POPL 1997 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/263699.263712)) | The untrusted producer ships evidence that the consumer checks cheaply, without trusting the producer. | The plan carries its goal, ordering, waits and optional step assertions; the kernel checks them, never the agent's reasoning. | Our plan carries hints, not a proof: the kernel recomputes everything. |
| **Certifying algorithms.** R. M. McConnell, K. Mehlhorn, S. Näher, P. Schweitzer, "Certifying algorithms", *Computer Science Review* 5(2), 2011 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cosrev.2010.09.009)) | An algorithm returns, with each answer, a witness that is easy to verify. | A Refuted verdict names the step, the rule and the state that breaks it, so a person can check the refutation by hand. | Proved has no comparably small witness: you trust the kernel's run. |
| **Translation validation.** A. Pnueli, M. Siegel, E. Singerman, "Translation Validation", TACAS 1998 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/BFb0054170)) | Instead of proving a compiler correct once, check each translation it produces. | The executor compiles each step to API calls; `executor.CheckTranslation` compares what it actually sent, from the API audit log, with what the plan compiled to (C-17). | We check the executor's writes after the run, not before; a wrong write is detected, not prevented ([GAP-01](known-gaps.md#gap-01-the-executor-can-patch-any-field-of-a-node-or-deployment)). |

## 3. What a plan means

| Public work | The idea | What planproof takes | Where we fall short |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Hoare logic.** C. A. R. Hoare, "An Axiomatic Basis for Computer Programming", *CACM* 12(10), 1969 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/363235.363259)) | Reason about a program with preconditions and postconditions on each step. | Each operation has a rule with what must hold before it and what it changes (spec 03); a plan may add `pre` and `post` assertions to steps. | - |
| **The frame problem.** J. McCarthy and P. Hayes, "Some Philosophical Problems from the Standpoint of Artificial Intelligence", *Machine Intelligence 4*, 1969 ([overview](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/frame-problem/)) | Describing what an action changes is easy; saying everything it leaves alone is the hard part. | Rule `frame`: the plan's total effect must equal its stated goal exactly. Anything the steps change that the goal doesn't list is refuted. This is what catches "and also quietly updated cart". | Our frame covers only the fields the model tracks; changes outside the model are out of scope (spec 02). |
| **Abstract interpretation.** P. Cousot and R. Cousot, POPL 1977 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/512950.512973)) | Run the program on an abstract, conservative picture of its state to learn facts that hold for every real run. | The "board game": pods are ready on a node, pending, or somewhere in a set of nodes; a drain counts the worst case. Unknown when the picture can't decide. | Our abstraction is hand-made, not derived from a formal semantics of Kubernetes; the fidelity tests (C area) are our only evidence it's conservative. |
| **Plan validation in AI planning.** R. Howey, D. Long, M. Fox, "VAL: automatic plan validation, continuous effects and mixed initiative planning using PDDL", ICTAI 2004 ([record](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/2550/)) | A separate tool checks a planner's output against the domain model, so planners never have to be trusted. Used to judge the International Planning Competition. | The closest existing tool to what planproof does: validate a sequence of actions against preconditions, effects and invariants. | VAL checks against a model written in a standard language (PDDL); ours is Go code. Writing the Kubernetes model in PDDL, or in Lean (phase 5), would let others check it with their own tools. |

## 4. Language models with a verifier

| Public work | The idea | What planproof takes | Where we fall short |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **LLM-Modulo frameworks.** S. Kambhampati et al., "Position: LLMs Can't Plan, But Can Help Planning in LLM-Modulo Frameworks", ICML 2024 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01817)) | Language models guess plans; sound external verifiers check them and send back critiques. | Exactly this loop: the model guesses, the kernel checks, the facts go back, the model repairs (agent loop, E-02). Our trials found the same thing: the verifier, not the model, is what makes the output safe. | Our verifier covers one narrow domain, and our task set is small (13 tasks). |
| **ReAct** (S. Yao et al., 2022, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.03629)) and **Reflexion** (N. Shinn et al., 2023, [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11366)) | Models act through tools and improve from feedback in later attempts. | One tool, `submit_plan`, and the kernel's reply as the feedback. | - |
| **Cedar.** "How We Built Cedar: A Verification-Guided Approach", 2024 ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01688); [cedarpolicy.com](https://www.cedarpolicy.com/)) | Model the language in Lean, prove properties of the model, and differentially test the production code against it. | The phase 5 plan, copied: a Lean model of planproof's semantics, a soundness proof, and differential tests against the Go kernel. Until then, our fidelity tests compare the model with a real cluster instead. | Not done yet. |
| **Automated reasoning at AWS.** C. Newcombe et al., "How Amazon Web Services Uses Formal Methods", *CACM* 58(4), 2015 ([page](https://www.amazon.science/publications/how-amazon-web-services-uses-formal-methods)); J. Backes et al., "Semantic-based Automated Reasoning for AWS Access Policies using SMT" (Zelkova), FMCAD 2018 ([page](https://www.amazon.science/publications/semantic-based-automated-reasoning-for-aws-access-policies-using-smt)) | Formal methods pay off in industry, for designs (TLA+) and at scale for checking configurations (Zelkova, millions of checks a day). | The goal: a checker cheap enough to run on every change. | Ours is a lab project, not a production service. |

## 5. Operations practice

| Public work | The idea | What planproof takes | Where we fall short |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Change is the main cause of outages.** *Site Reliability Engineering* (Google, 2016), "Change Management": "roughly 70% of outages are due to changes in a live system" ([chapter](https://sre.google/sre-book/introduction/)) | Make changes progressive, detect problems fast, roll back safely. | Why check plans at all; every plan has a checked rollback (rule `rollback`). | We check before the change; we don't do progressive rollout or detection. |
| **The S3 outage of 28 February 2017** ([AWS summary](https://aws.amazon.com/message/41926/)) | A mistyped input to a capacity-removal command took out too many servers. AWS changed the tool to remove capacity more slowly and to refuse to take a subsystem below its minimum required capacity. | That safeguard, as rules: `budget`, `never-zero` and `capacity` check every intermediate state of the plan, and `limits` caps how many nodes may stop taking pods. | - |
| **Kubernetes' own safety mechanisms**: [disruption budgets](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/workloads/pods/disruptions/), the [Eviction API](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/scheduling-eviction/api-eviction/) and [safe drains](https://kubernetes.io/docs/tasks/administer-cluster/safely-drain-node/), [dry runs and resource versions](https://kubernetes.io/docs/reference/using-api/api-concepts/), [audit logging](https://kubernetes.io/docs/tasks/debug/debug-cluster/audit/) | The cluster refuses an eviction that breaks a budget, at the moment of the eviction. Optimistic concurrency stops a write based on stale state. | The kernel checks budgets for the whole plan in advance; the executor drains only through the Eviction API (so the cluster checks again), writes with resourceVersion preconditions, and is audited. | The kernel predicts; Kubernetes enforces. When they disagree, Kubernetes wins, and the executor stops. |
| **Plan, then apply.** [`terraform plan`](https://developer.hashicorp.com/terraform/cli/commands/plan), `kubectl diff` | Show the change before making it; apply exactly what was shown. | Approval is bound to the plan, snapshot, policy and kernel hashes; the executor refuses anything else. | - |
| **Policy as code.** [Open Policy Agent](https://www.openpolicyagent.org/docs/latest/), [Gatekeeper](https://open-policy-agent.github.io/gatekeeper/website/docs/) | Rules written as code, evaluated on each request. | `safe-ops-agent-k8s` uses OPA on each single change; planproof checks a whole multi-step plan over time. The two are compared in [results/safeops-comparison.md](../results/safeops-comparison.md). | Our policy language is much less expressive than Rego ([GAP-06](known-gaps.md#gap-06-the-policy-cant-forbid-scale-to-zero-for-one-deployment-only)). |

## 6. Writing for the person who approves

| Public work | The idea | What planproof takes | Where we fall short |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English** ([ASD](https://www.asd-europe.org/standards-specifications/simplified-technical-english/)) | A controlled language for maintenance documents: a limited dictionary, one meaning per word, short sentences, one instruction per sentence. | The review packet is written in an STE-style subset: templates as data, a word list (`render/words.txt`), and a linter that rejects any template using a word not on the list. | Ours is inspired by STE, not certified against it. |
| **Plain language** ([Plain Writing Act of 2010](https://www.plainlanguage.gov/law/); [Federal Plain Language Guidelines](https://centerforplainlanguage.org/learning-training/tools-training/)) | Write for the reader: their task first, common words, short sentences. | The packet starts with the goal and the result, and says plainly what to do ("Do not approve this plan"). | - |
| **Automation bias.** R. Parasuraman and D. H. Manzey, "Complacency and Bias in Human Use of Automation", *Human Factors* 52(3), 2010 ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720810376055)) | People over-trust automated aids, experts included, and training doesn't prevent it. | The packet always lists what was **not** checked, starting with "The kernel does not check that this goal is the correct goal for the request. You must check this." The reviewer study (E-22) measures whether people still miss wrong goals. | Whether the packet reduces automation bias, or adds to it, is exactly what the reviewer study has to find out with people. |
| **Code review in practice.** C. Sadowski et al., "Modern Code Review: A Case Study at Google", ICSE-SEIP 2018 ([page](https://research.google/pubs/modern-code-review-a-case-study-at-google/)) | Review is a lightweight human check, focused on understanding the change. | The packet is designed for a short review: the goal fits on a few lines. | - |

## 7. Our own earlier work

**safe-ops-agent-k8s** ([repository](https://github.com/billykong/safe-ops-agent-k8s); [write-up](https://billykong.github.io/defence-in-depth/)): the agent holds no credentials, proposes changes as data, and a small non-AI controller applies only what a person approved, bound by a hash. planproof keeps all of that and replaces the per-change policy gate with a checker for whole plans. [results/safeops-comparison.md](../results/safeops-comparison.md) shows what each catches.
